The Fermi–Pasta–Ulam–Tsingou Problem

A chain of masses connected by weakly nonlinear springs is set vibrating in a single pure tone. According to statistical mechanics, the energy should gradually spread across the system’s normal modes.

But it doesn’t.

Instead, the energy repeatedly flows back toward the mode where it started — producing a striking phenomenon known as Fermi–Pasta–Ulam–Tsingou recurrence.

The FPUT Experiment Explained

This interactive simulation is a browser port of a Fortran FPUT solver. It uses the same underlying force law, F = k(y′ + αy′²), which adds a small quadratic nonlinear correction to an otherwise ordinary spring.

The simulation also uses the fourth-order Yoshida symplectic integrator, allowing the system to evolve while preserving the Hamiltonian structure of the dynamics.

Start the chain in a single low-frequency normal mode and watch what happens: instead of settling into an even distribution of energy, the system develops a pattern of transfers and recurrences between its modes.

These are the same results that Fermi, Pasta, Ulam, and Tsingou encountered in 1955 while running numerical experiments on the MANIAC computer.

What was expected to be a simple demonstration of thermalization became one of the foundational surprises of nonlinear dynamics: a system can be nonlinear and still resist the march toward equilibrium.

In Loving Memory of Mary Tsingou

In the summer of 1953, Enrico Fermi, John Pasta, Stanislaw Ulam and Mary Tsingou set out to study the behavior of a chain of masses on weakly nonlinear springs. They published their results in a Los Alamos report in 1955. The resulting phenomenon was named the Fermi–Pasta–Ulam problem, notably leaving Mary Tsingou's contributions unrecognized. However, in 2008, Thierry Dauxois published an article in Physics Today that highlighted her contributions to the problem. Following this, the problem was renamed the Fermi–Pasta–Ulam–Tsingou problem in her honor.

Mary Tsingou earned her undergraduate degree from the University of Wisconsin and a Master's degree in Applied Mathematics from the University of Michigan. In 1952, she joined the Los Alamos National Laboratory, where she dedicated her entire 35-year career. Selected as one of the first programmers for the historic MANIAC computer, she spent her career solving complex problems in mathematics and applied physics. During her time at Los Alamos, she made significant contributions to the FPUT problem, the Star Wars program (Strategic Defense Initiative), and was an early expert in Fortran.

As one of the first people to use computers to study complex systems, her work on the FPUT problem helped to lay the foundation for the field of chaos theory. Today, we remember Mary Tsingou for her pioneering work in the field of nonlinear dynamics and her many contributions in computing. This website is dedicated to her memory.
